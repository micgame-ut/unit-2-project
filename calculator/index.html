What your chatbot use actually costs

Enter an average day of AI use and this tool adds it up into one carbon and water cost, set against your own footprint and the everyday things around it. Numbers for AI's energy and water come from EcoLogits, an open-source estimator of the energy, carbon, and water behind each AI model. The Claude numbers are the shakiest here: Anthropic hasn't said how Claude is built, so EcoLogits has to guess, and its guess might run low (more in the methodology). For carbon I use the average grid intensity of the region you pick (not EcoLogits' single whole-world average) plus the hardware's embodied emissions; everything else is sourced in the methodology below. Training is excluded as too uncertain, but split across all users it adds little to your personal footprint. For a fuller carbon picture, and why grid-level changes swamp everything else, see my visual here.

What it writes

"Typical output" is how long each reply is. A "coding / agent session" means one full run of an AI coding assistant that writes and edits code across many steps. It uses EcoLogits' 100,000-token "assist application development" benchmark (much of it the model's own reasoning and tool calls, not final code).

For context, the finished code for this page is about 29,000 tokens, and it took roughly 250,000 tokens of Opus 4.8 output to build it through all the drafts. About the energy of running a dishwasher, or playing a PS5 for 5-6 hours.

How these numbers are made

Per-model AI estimates. The energy, carbon, and water numbers for each prompt come from the open-source EcoLogits library (v0.10), the same code that runs the EcoLogits calculator. The model list is current as of June 2026. EcoLogits estimates electricity from how big each model is (really, how many parameters it uses on each token) and how long the reply is, then adds the cost of manufacturing the hardware. The low and high ends are EcoLogits' own 95% confidence interval, which mostly comes down to nobody outside these companies knowing how big the closed models really are. One of its guesses matters a lot here: Anthropic has never said how Claude is built, and EcoLogits' model data assumes Claude Opus and Sonnet are mixture-of-experts models, which would mean they only use a fraction of their parameters on any given token (it marks every Claude entry "model-arch-not-released"). People who track this disagree. Some think the clues we do have, like Claude's speed and pricing, point to dense models that use every parameter every time. Others think Claude is a very large mixture-of-experts. If the dense camp is right, the Claude numbers on this page are probably too low. The water numbers count consumption, not withdrawal: water that is actually gone, evaporated cooling the data center or generating its electricity, not water taken in and then returned. Every water figure on this page works this way, so the comparisons are apples to apples.

Carbon basis. EcoLogits gives each prompt's electricity use and its embodied (hardware-manufacturing) emissions separately. We keep EcoLogits' embodied figure as-is and cost the electricity on the grid where you live, using carbon intensities from Ember 2024 and Our World in Data: roughly 380 (US), 215 (EU), 125 (UK), 580 (China), 700 (India), and 480 (world) g CO2e/kWh. Water is left on EcoLogits' global basis.

Image and video generation. These rows use energy per output from two studies, costed on your region's grid like the text prompts. Images use one figure for any tool: about 2.9 Wh per image, measured by Luccioni, Jernite & Strubell 2024 (FAccT) as 2.907 kWh per 1,000 images on a single hardware setup (8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs). The authors say this is not representative of every deployment, and it predates newer image models, so treat it as an order of magnitude, not a per-tool number; the study gives no range, so none is shown. Video uses per-model, per-clip figures from Jegham, Gamazaychikov & Luccioni 2026, at the clip length and resolution the paper reports for each model, which differ, so compare models with care. The open models (LTX-2 at about 2.95 Wh for a 3-second 1024p clip, HunyuanVideo-1.5 at about 478.5 Wh for an 8-second clip on 8 GPUs) were measured directly on real GPUs, with under 3% error. The proprietary models (8-second, 720p clips) could not be measured, so the authors estimated them from API response times and assumed hardware, and give a range: Veo 3 19.8–43.4 Wh, Seedance-1 72.1–87.9 Wh, Gen-4.5 241.6–410.9 Wh, and Sora 2.0 Pro 315.1–534.4 Wh. The typical value shown here is the middle of each range; that choice is ours, not the paper's. Energy grows faster than clip length or resolution, so a longer or sharper clip can cost far more than these. Water is not estimated for images or video: no peer-reviewed study has measured it, and deriving one from a data-center water-use rate we could not verify would overstate how sure we are. No embodied-hardware figure is published for either, so their carbon is electricity only, and training is excluded as it is everywhere else on this page.

Typical or range, and "what if Claude is dense?" The Numbers switch at the top shows every AI and digital-habit figure, and their bars in the charts, either as one typical value or as its low-to-high range, so you can see how uncertain each one is. For the AI rows the range is EcoLogits' own 95% interval; for images and streaming no range is published, so those stay a single figure; the other habits and video models use their sources' ranges. Project totals are always a range. The reference items in the charts (a burger, a flight) are single published figures. The dense checkbox appears when Claude Opus 4.8 or Sonnet 4.6 is in use. In EcoLogits' model data, both are mixture-of-experts models: Opus 670 billion parameters with 67–200 billion active on each token, Sonnet 440 billion with 44–132 billion active (10–30%), and its low and high estimates use the fewest and the most active. EcoLogits' energy and water rise in a straight line with active parameters (its typical value sits exactly halfway between its low and high), so the dense estimate extends that same line to every parameter active. That comes to about 3 times the typical energy for Sonnet and 3.5 times for Opus, and about 4 times the water; the hardware-manufacturing (embodied) share does not change. It is well short of 3.3–10 times, because part of each reply's energy does not grow with model size. Claude Haiku 4.5 is left alone, because EcoLogits already treats it as dense (10–35 billion parameters). With both switches on, the range runs from EcoLogits' low estimate to the dense estimate, covering both views of how Claude is built. The parameter counts are EcoLogits' estimates, not figures from Anthropic.

Your digital habits. Streaming, video calls, and gaming are a separate total, never added to your AI figures; each one you enter gets its own bar in the daily and yearly charts, and your own gaming replaces the generic "hour on a PS5" line. Each source reports grams on one particular grid, so we convert back to electricity using that grid and then cost it on the grid for your region, the same way as the AI rows. Streaming uses Kamiya / IEA 2020: about 0.077 kWh an hour for the average mix of devices (36 g CO2 on the global-average grid; figures as republished by Carbon Brief). That analysis also corrected a widely repeated claim of 3.2 kg an hour, which was about 90 times too high. It is from 2020, and bitrates and grids have shifted since. Institutional source, transparent method. The device matters: Kamiya puts 72% of streaming's energy in the viewing device (23% network, 5% data centres) and says a 50-inch TV uses about 100 times the electricity of a phone and 5 times a laptop. He gives ratios, not per-device amounts, so we treat the average as a laptop and scale only the device's 72% share, leaving network and data-centre energy as they are: a phone comes to about a third of a laptop and a TV to about four times, per hour. Treating the average as a laptop is our assumption. Video calls use Mortas 2026: 10.2–21.6 g CO2e an hour with the camera on and 4.8–10.8 g with it off, measured on France's low-carbon grid (79.1 g/kWh), so they come out several times higher on most other grids. The typical value is the middle of each range. The author calls this preliminary: 9 meetings on one laptop and one connection, with no screen sharing. Gaming uses power measured during actual gameplay by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory for the California Energy Commission (Mills et al. 2019, CEC-500-2019-042; also Mills et al. 2019), which tested 26 systems across dozens of games. Each device's typical value is the average of the systems in that group and its range runs from the lowest to the highest, read from the report's chart of per-system averages: a regular PC (entry-level desktops) about 108 W (45–183 W), a gaming PC (mid-range and high-end desktops) about 234 W (127–328 W), and an older console (PS4 and Xbox One models) about 89 W (60–128 W). That hardware is from 2016, and power swings widely with the game being played. Berkeley Lab did not test current consoles, so a PS5 or Xbox Series X uses NRDC's 2021 measurements of about 160–200 W (NRDC), which we could see only through news coverage of it, because NRDC's page blocked our access; treat that one as unverified. The yearly totals sometimes quoted for gaming (for example about 1,400 kWh a year for a gaming PC) are not used here, because they mix in hours of use and idle time. None of these sources measures water, so habits are carbon only. Social media is left out: we found no source that quantifies it, and this page does not guess.

Project totals. Coding-agent work comes in irregular bursts, so the project section counts whole sessions instead of a daily habit and is never added to the daily or yearly figures. One session is EcoLogits' 100,000-output-token "assist application development" benchmark, the same one behind the "coding / agent session" row. The range starts at EcoLogits' low estimate and ends at twice its high estimate. The doubling comes from Bai et al. 2026 (Microsoft Research, a preprint), who ran 8 frontier models on 500 real software tasks (SWE-bench Verified) and found the most expensive run of the same task typically cost about twice the cheapest. Their extreme cases reached around 30 times, but that is the tail, not the norm, so it is not built into the range. They also found that how hard people rated a task barely predicted its cost, and that the agents themselves underestimated their own usage. So treat the range as typical, not a ceiling. Benchmark tasks may also differ from your real work. The familiar comparison uses the same sourced reference items as the charts on this page.

Output, words, and code. Each row maps to an EcoLogits output-token count: tweet (50), short email (170), article summary (250), chatbot reply (400), 5-page report (5,000), long document (15,000), coding or agent session (100,000), and re-writing the Lord of the Rings trilogy (500,000) — both EcoLogits benchmark tasks; the trilogy itself runs about 480,000 words. The coding/agent session uses EcoLogits' 100,000-token "assist application development" benchmark, which represents one full run that writes and edits code across many steps. Cost scales with length, so long generations dominate. Counts are per day; annual figures multiply by 365. Words assume OpenAI's ~0.75 words per token, read at 238 words a minute (Brysbaert 2019). A coding session is counted as lines of code (~10 tokens a line) rather than words to read. Only about 15% of its output tokens are treated as actual code; the rest is reasoning (which dominates for reasoning models), tool calls, file exploration, and explanation, so a 100,000-token session is on the order of 1,500 lines. This is a rough estimate with wide uncertainty.

Your footprint. "Where you live" is a regional baseline for goods, services, and shared infrastructure (per-capita consumption from Our World in Data); home energy and driving are separate so they do not double-count. Home runs from a small apartment to a big house, about 1.5 to 7 t CO2e a year (EIA RECS; Goldstein et al. 2020). Driving uses EPA's ~400 g CO2/mile across roughly 3,000 to 25,000 miles a year (FHWA). Diet uses food footprints from Poore & Nemecek (2018) and Scarborough et al. (2023): about 1.05 t CO2e a year for a vegan diet up to 3.2 t for a heavy-meat one. Flying is anchored to a transatlantic round trip of about 1.6 t (Wynes & Nicholas 2017); the rarely / sometimes / often options are roughly 0.5, 1.5, and 5 such trips a year. The water footprint uses blue water only — freshwater actually drawn from rivers, lakes, and aquifers. We deliberately leave out green water (rain that falls on farmland and would evaporate anyway, whether or not a crop is grown) and grey water (a notional pollution-dilution volume), because neither is a real draw on freshwater supplies. The AI figure is already blue (data-center cooling and power-plant water), so both sides match. The per-person blue footprint is from the Water Footprint Network (Mekonnen & Hoekstra): about 450 m3/yr for the US and 153 m3 globally (the other regions are scaled by their irrigation intensity and are approximate). Removing green water changes the picture a lot: most of a food's water is rain, so a beef burger drops from ~1,700 L total to about 6 L of freshwater, while irrigated crops stay high.

Lifestyle cuts (the yearly chart). The "saved" figures come from the Founders Pledge Climate & Lifestyle report, drawing on Wynes & Nicholas (2017) (living car-free 2.4 t, avoiding a transatlantic flight 1.6 t, hang-drying, recycling, LED bulbs) and Ivanova et al. (2020) (green electricity, heat-pump heating, a home retrofit, electric and hybrid cars).

Ways you add emissions (the yearly chart). Manufacturing a new car ≈ 6 t CO2e (Berners-Lee, How Bad Are Bananas?); a new flat-screen TV ≈ 350 kg; a new laptop ≈ 250 kg; a new bicycle ≈ 100 kg and a new sofa ≈ 90 kg. Flights, per passenger (myclimate, with a transatlantic round trip ≈ 1.6 t per Wynes & Nicholas): a short-haul round trip ≈ 250 kg, a round-trip US cross-country flight ≈ 1 t, and a transatlantic round trip ≈ 1.6 t. A year of average US driving (~12,000 mi) ≈ 4.8 t at ~0.40 kg/mile (EPA). A beef burger every week for a year ≈ 156 kg (Poore & Nemecek, ~3 kg each); a year of daily coffee ≈ 77 kg; a cotton T-shirt ≈ 7 kg; a new smartphone ≈ 70 kg (Apple); and a pair of jeans ≈ 33 kg (Levi's).

Everyday things. A cup of coffee: ~0.21 kg CO2e and 140 L of water. An hour on a PS5 (~200 W); 3 minutes in a 1,200 W microwave; a mile in a gas car (EPA, ~0.40 kg); a 10-minute hot shower (~21 gal, EPA WaterSense, plus water heating); a dishwasher load (≤3.5 gal, ENERGY STAR); a dryer load (~3 kWh); printing a 400-page book (~2.7 kg, Wells 2012) with paper's water footprint; a beef burger (~3 kg, Poore & Nemecek; ~1,700 L at 15,400 L/kg beef); a new smartphone (~70 kg, Apple) needing ~12,800 L; a pair of jeans (~33 kg, Levi's) at ~10,000 L (Water Footprint Network).

How the appliance figures are derived. The coffee, PS5, microwave, shower, dishwasher, and dryer carbon numbers are each that item's measured electricity (cited above) times the US grid (~0.38 kg CO2/kWh), so they fall on cleaner grids. Two depend heavily on assumptions: a cup of coffee is ~0.05 kg black up to ~0.5 kg for a large latte (we use ~0.2 kg), and a 10-minute hot shower is roughly 0.2 kg on a gas water heater up to 1.5 kg on electric resistance (we use ~0.7 kg). Where a single authority does not exist, figures are rounded mid-range estimates from the sources cited, not false precision.

Water comparisons (blue water). The water charts use the blue (freshwater) component only, so the comparison items are the irrigation- and process-water heavy ones, from the Water Footprint Network and crop studies: a beef burger (~6 L, since beef is ~90% rainwater), a cup of coffee (~4 L), a slice of bread (~8 L), an egg (~15 L), a glass of milk (~22 L), a bowl of rice (~25 L), an avocado (~60 L), a handful of almonds (~120 L, as each California almond takes ~12 L of irrigation), a cotton T-shirt (~1,500 L of its 2,700 L total is blue), a pair of jeans (~5,000 L), and a new smartphone (mostly ultrapure freshwater). The savings come from cutting irrigated products (a few fewer cotton clothes) and outdoor watering: a typical lawn drinks roughly 20,000 gal of freshwater a year, so letting it go unwatered or replacing it with native plants saves most of that (EPA WaterSense). Eating less meat barely moves the blue total, because almost all of meat's water is rain. Energy and manufactured goods have a blue-water cost too: thermoelectric power plants evaporate about 0.47 gal of freshwater per kWh (NREL, cooling/consumption only), so an hour of central air conditioning (~3 kWh) consumes roughly 1.4 gal and a day of the average US home's electricity (~29 kWh) about 14 gal — the very same power-plant cooling water that makes up much of AI's footprint. Printing a 300-page book uses on the order of ~5 gal of process freshwater (paper's water footprint is mostly rain on trees, with the blue mill water lower since most is returned to rivers).

Embed this calculator on your site

Paste this anywhere HTML is allowed and the whole calculator appears on your page, no credit required. The small script keeps the frame matched to the calculator's height as people use it; without it you just get a fixed-height scrolling frame, which also works. By default the embed follows each visitor's light or dark system setting; add ?theme=light or ?theme=dark to the address in src to force one look. 

<iframe src="https://andymasley.com/visuals/ai-prompt-footprint/embed"
  title="What does your AI use cost? An interactive calculator by Andy Masley"
  style="width:100%;border:0;" height="3000" loading="lazy"></iframe>
<script>
  window.addEventListener('message', function (e) {
    if (e.origin !== 'https://andymasley.com') return;
    if (!e.data || e.data.type !== 'aipf:height') return;
    document.querySelectorAll('iframe').forEach(function (f) {
      if (f.src.indexOf('https://andymasley.com/visuals/ai-prompt-footprint/embed') === 0) f.height = e.data.height;
    });
  });
</script>
